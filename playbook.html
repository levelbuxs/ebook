Step one

Pick the name

Lock this before you shoot anything — the @handle goes in the corner of every proof photo and every video, so picking later means reshoots. Click any card to shortlist it; it copies the whole set (brand, handle, member noun, hashtag) to your clipboard.

My pick is Whitelisted / @imwhitelisted, and the reason is the member noun: someone can say "I'm Whitelisted" out loud and it means something without revealing anything. It describes being approved, which is exactly what they're buying — not the free stuff, the access.

How to choose in 60 seconds: say each name out loud as if you're telling a friend at a party. If you have to explain it, cut it. Then check the handle is free on TikTok and Instagram before you fall in love with it — the handle matters more than the domain now.
You're not naming a website — you're naming a club. Two different words: the brand (the account people follow) and the member noun (the thing someone says about themselves). The member noun is the one that matters. When somebody can say "I'm ___" out loud, they stop being a customer and start being a member — and members defend you in the comments for free.

Words to avoid — they give it away: sampler, tester, reviewer, panel, insider club, sample, box, review club, freebie, VIP program. Any one of these and a curious person can find the program in one search. Names about access (whitelisted, cleared, on the list) protect you. Names about free stuff don't reveal anything either — the danger is only the mechanism words.
Handle backup trick: if the clean handle is taken, add a word that sounds native, not desperate — hq, club, daily, co, files. Avoid numbers and underscores. @imwhitelisted > @whitelisted_47 every time.

Step two

Section starter text

The first line of every section decides whether the rest of it gets read. Each block below is the job of that section, then three openers you can steal. Brackets are fill-in-the-blanks.

The hero / above the fold

Job: create disbelief in under 3 seconds. Do not explain anything yet.

A — The receipt opener (strongest)

“Random stuff worth up to $[699] keeps showing up at my door for $0.00. Not a coupon. Not a glitch I found on Reddit. Last [Tuesday] it was a [exact item], and I've got the receipt to prove it.”

Leads with a number and a claim, then immediately offers evidence. Use this one.

B — The anti-coupon opener

“Coupons are a 20% off hobby. This is a 100% off hobby. Every few days something shows up at my house and the receipt says zero.”

Best if your ad is running against coupon/savings audiences. It reframes instantly.

C — The confession opener

“I need to say upfront this sounds like a scam. It isn't, and I'm not going to argue about it — just watch the video where I do it without cutting the screen.”

Names the objection in the first line. Very high trust, very high scroll-through.
The “how it works” section

Job: make it feel easy AND make step 2 feel like a secret.

A

“It's three steps, and you already know step one. Step two is the one everybody skips, which is why most people try this once, get nothing, and decide it's fake.”

Tells them they're smart, then tells them they're missing something. Works every time.

B

“There's an order to this. Four things, in sequence. Do them out of order and nothing arrives — no error, no email, just silence. That's the part nobody writes down.”

Explains why they failed in the past without calling them dumb.

C

“Ten minutes to learn. About three minutes to do. Then you wait a few days and a box shows up. That's the whole thing — there's no part two where I upsell you.”

Kills the “this will be a huge time sink” objection.
The proof section

Job: shut down “pics or it didn't happen” before they think it.

A

“Screenshots, not stories. These are all off my own phone — order totals, delivery confirmations, and the stuff actually sitting in my house right now.”

“Screenshots, not stories” is the line. Short and rhythmic.

B

“I'm blurring my address because I'm not an idiot. Everything else is exactly as it came off my screen.”

Pre-empting the “why is it blurred” suspicion makes the unblurred parts more credible.

C

“Photos can be faked, so here's the uncut screen recording — start to finish, no edits, including the boring part where I'm waiting for the page to load.”

The “boring part” detail is what makes it believable. Never cut it.
The “what's inside” section

Job: make the ebook feel fast to finish and specific.

A

“Short on purpose. You should finish it tonight and have your first claim in before bed. If it were 200 pages I could charge more, but nobody finishes 200 pages.”

Turns shortness from a weakness into the feature. Also justifies not charging more.

B

“No fluff chapter about mindset. Chapter 1 gets you to your first claim. Chapter 7 is a one-page cheat sheet you'll tape inside a cabinet.”

Promises no filler — the #1 fear when buying any ebook.

C

“Every chapter is a thing you do, not a thing you learn. You'll be mid-claim by page 15.”

Action framing. Great for skimmers.
The price section

Job: make the price feel small next to the value, without fake urgency.

A

“$27. That's less than the last thing you bought on Amazon and forgot about. One successful claim pays for it [20] times over.”

Concrete comparison to their own behavior, not to abstract value.

B

“I priced it at $27 because that's below the point where people need to think about it. I'd rather you just try it.”

Honest pricing rationale reads as confident, not salesy.

C

“It's $27 today. It goes to $[47] when I finish the [update/alerts] section — the price on the button is the real price, and if you buy now you keep the updates free.”

Real, specific urgency. Only say this if it's true.
The close

Job: one last small push. Never beg.

A

“You'll spend more than $27 on stuff you don't even want this month. Ten minutes from now you'll know how to do this — or you'll keep scrolling. Both are fine.”

Gives them an out, which paradoxically raises conversion.

B

“Worst case you're out $27 and you know the trick. Best case a $[499] [thing] shows up next week. Those are the actual two outcomes.”

Frames risk as tiny by naming both outcomes plainly.

C

“If it doesn't work you email me, I send your money back, and you keep the book. I can offer that because almost nobody asks.”

The most honest version of a guarantee — and it converts better than the legalistic one.
Step three

Ad hooks

First 3 seconds. The rule: one weird, specific, true sentence. No “are you tired of…”, no “in today's video”, no “you won't believe”.

“A $499 espresso machine showed up at my house on Thursday. I paid nothing.”

“This is the fourth thing this month that cost me zero dollars.”

“My neighbor asked if I'd started stealing. I just learned about the drop thing.”

“Stop clipping coupons. The version of this where the total is literally zero is easier.”

“I'm not saying 'free stuff' like a scammer, I'm showing you a receipt that says $0.00.”

“Three steps. Everyone knows step one. Almost nobody knows step two exists.”

“The reason nobody talks about this is that every person who learns it is one more person taking the items.”

“I got a pizza oven for free and I don't even like pizza that much.”

“You're going to assume this is fake. Fine. Watch me do it without cutting.”

“Day 61 of getting things delivered for $0. Today it's a robot vacuum.”

Step four

Full ad copy

Three lengths. Same offer, same structure: hook → proof → the missed step → the ask.

Short (TikTok / Reels / Stories · ~15 sec)

“A $499 espresso machine showed up at my door last Thursday and I paid zero dollars.

It's not a coupon — coupons take 20% off, this takes 100% off.

Three steps. Everyone knows step one. Nobody knows step two exists, which is why people try this once, get nothing, and call it fake.

I wrote it all down. It's $27. Link's right there.”

On-screen text idea: put “$0.00” on screen the entire time, small, in the corner.
Medium (Facebook / IG feed · 60–90 words)

“Random stuff worth up to $699 keeps showing up at my house and the receipt says $0.00.

Last week it was a robot vacuum. Before that, an air fryer. Before that, something I genuinely can't explain.

It's not coupons, it's not cashback, and it's not a glitch that'll get patched next week. There's a set of places giving items away on purpose — you just have to know where to look and the exact order to claim in.

That order is the whole secret. Get it wrong and nothing arrives. No error, no email, just nothing. That's why most people quit after one try.

I wrote it down in a short ebook. $27, instant download, refund if nothing shows up in 30 days.”

The middle paragraph is doing the work — it names and dismisses the three things they'll assume this is.
Long (email / blog / sales page body)

“I need to open by saying this sounds like a scam, and I get it. If I saw this ad I'd scroll past too.

So here's the receipt instead of a pitch: last Thursday, a $499 espresso machine. Total paid: $0.00. The week before, a $279 robot vacuum. Same total.

Here's what it isn't. It isn't coupons — I don't clip anything. It isn't cashback — I'm not waiting for 3% to dribble into an account. It isn't a glitch somebody found and posted to Reddit that'll be dead by Friday.

What it actually is: a handful of places give items away on purpose. They don't advertise it, because the moment everybody knows, the items are gone. Finding them takes about ten minutes to learn.

But — and this is the whole thing — there's an order to claiming. Four steps, in sequence. Miss one and nothing shows up. No error message, no rejection email. Just silence. That's why almost everybody who tries this gives up after one attempt and tells their friends it's fake. They're not lying. They just did it out of order.

I wrote the sequence down, along with where to look, how to tell a real drop from a waste of time, and the mistakes that get people shut out. It's 48 pages, mostly screenshots.

$27. If you try it twice and nothing arrives, email me and I'll refund you — and you keep the book.”

The “here's what it isn't” paragraph is the highest-leverage paragraph in the whole ad. Never cut it.
